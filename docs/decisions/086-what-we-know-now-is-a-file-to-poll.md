# 086 — What we know now is published as a file to poll; nothing is pushed until a subscriber asks

**Status:** accepted · **Decided:** 2026-10-21, by Peter · **Work:** GitHub [#738](https://github.com/salish-sea/salishsea-io/issues/738) (the file), [#739](https://github.com/salish-sea/salishsea-io/issues/739) (serving our own sightings to a port system, and the license) · **Context:** the Marine Exchange of Puget Sound's request of 2026-10-21 for a sightings layer in PortLink · **Relates:** [003](003-dwc-export-pipeline.md) (the archive this complements), [056](056-the-logged-out-read-path-is-built-as-static-files.md) (the day files and the manifest), [080](080-a-sighting-is-unconfirmed-until-someone-else-agrees.md) (the confirmation status the file carries), [docs/rights-policy.md](../rights-policy.md) (why Maplify's time is a report time, and the native license it claims)

## Context

The Darwin Core archive serves people who want the record: GBIF, researchers, anyone asking what was seen over years. A different kind of consumer wants to know what is happening now: a vessel-alerting tool, a notifier for shore watchers, anyone deciding where to look or where not to steer in the next hour. The question was whether to complement the archive with an interface for them, and the first idea was a data file plus an SNS topic.

The day the question was asked, a consumer asked too. The Marine Exchange of Puget Sound runs PortLink with Wärtsilä, live since 2026-09-22: the port community's shared picture of vessel calls and AIS traffic. Its director wants whale sightings as a layer beside the ships, the Coast Guard has asked the Exchange about an AIS-based alert for its Cetacean Desk, and the Exchange's developer will build the integration. Their questions are what data is available, in what format, how it is accessed, how often it updates and what its verification status means. Scott Veirs had already pointed them at Acartia's open endpoint, `https://acartia.io/api/v1/sightings/current`, as the short-term answer, and said he expects salishsea.io to replace Acartia in 2027. That expectation is Scott's and is recorded here as his; this project has not yet decided to be Acartia's successor.

That endpoint is therefore the contract such consumers already know. It takes no key and returns the last seven days of reports, by report date, as a flat JSON array: coordinates, a species label, a count, a `trusted` flag, the report time, the source's name and entity, a witness, comments and a photo URL. The Exchange's vessel traffic display and an earlier whale map both read it. On 2026-10-21 it held 46 reports, every one from Conserve.io's Whale Alert app, 41 of them trusted, reaching Acartia a median of six minutes after the report and in the worst case two days after.

Most of such an interface already exists here. The build rewrites the day files, `/read-path/days/<YYYY-MM-DD>.json`, within about five minutes of any change, and `/read-path/manifest.json` carries the time of the snapshot that produced them; the site itself polls the manifest every minute and refetches the day when it moves (decision 056). Nothing stops a downstream system doing the same today. What it lacks is a contract: the day files carry the frontend's own row shape, which 056 chose on purpose so that the files and the page parse the same thing, and that shape changes whenever the frontend needs it to. A public consumer that bound itself to it would either break when the site moved or freeze the site.

Two facts about currency shape what such an interface can honestly be.

First, almost everything we publish reaches us late, and one source does not record when the animals were seen at all. Maplify keeps a single timestamp, the moment it received the report; the rights policy's ALIGN-05 already refuses to emit it at better than day precision in the archive for that reason. We fetch Maplify every five minutes, so our copy of a Whale Alert report runs five to fifteen minutes behind Acartia's. Orcasound bouts are curated by a moderator hours or days after the fact (decision 013). iNaturalist observations are uploaded after the outing. Only our own contributors' sightings are recorded when they happen, and there are few of them. A feed that showed a Maplify report at second precision to a ship's bridge would assert a time we do not know.

Second, the region's real-time avoidance system already exists upstream of us. Orca Network's reports flow through Acartia into Ocean Wise's Whale Report Alert System (WRAS) and on to Conserve.io and Maplify, where we fetch them. Most of what a feed of ours would carry is that data coming back round. The strategy for community uptake says it plainly: avoid rebuilding what Acartia already does. The one thing we originate that WRAS never sees is our own contributors' sightings.

## Decision

**A file of recent occurrences, with a documented and versioned schema, written by the build beside the day files.** It holds the last seven days, Acartia's window, so a consumer of Acartia's endpoint can move to it without changing what it keeps, and it says when it was built. Each row carries what a consumer acting now needs and the day files leave implicit: the provider; whether the time is when the animals were observed or when the report arrived, which for every Maplify row is the report; the confirmation status of decision 080, standing where Acartia's `trusted` flag stood, so unconfirmed reports are flagged rather than dropped; the reported accuracy or null, never a made-up number; and a bout's end time. Every field Acartia's endpoint carries has a counterpart or a stated absence (#738 has the mapping). It is a separate file so that the frontend's shape and the public contract can move independently. Peter judged the Pacific-day boundary, which splits a night's reports across two day files, not a problem worth solving on its own; the window is for Acartia's consumers.

**The manifest is the change signal, and polling is the protocol.** The site already works this way, and it is what the consumer that has asked needs: an address to read, a rate to read it at, and a flag to filter on. Caddy answers conditional requests on these files, so a consumer that polls once a minute transfers nothing while nothing has changed.

**Nothing is pushed until a subscriber asks for push.** No SNS topic, no webhooks, no WebSub. The posture matches PRODUCT.md, which puts real-time push notifications out of scope for people; this extends it to machines, with the door open. An SNS topic in particular is cheap to run and awkward to consume: a subscriber needs an AWS identity or an HTTPS endpoint that completes a confirmation handshake, and the build runs on Fly, so publishing would mean widening the Fly machine's AWS credentials beyond the two buckets the store writer may touch today. When a consumer needs push, its needs choose the mechanism.

**The file says what it is not.** Its documentation states the latency of each source and that this is a view of what SalishSea.io has learned, minutes to days after the fact, not a ship-strike product. That is true of the map too, and the map's reader can see the timestamps; a machine's cannot unless told.

**What our own sightings are allowed to feed is #739's question**, deliberately not this file's. The rights policy says native records are CC-BY-NC, and the archive has emitted them to GBIF under that license since June 2026. Checking where that came from, on 2026-10-21, found that nobody granted it: the policy cites a requirements document that has never existed in this repository, the "stated platform policy" its consent basis relies on was never written, and the notice it promised on the submission form never shipped. The only license a contributor chooses is for their photos, and it defaults to CC-BY. So there is no license on our own sightings yet, and the archive has been asserting one. The question is the first grant, not a relicense, and Peter's view is that it should be CC-BY. Agreement is asked in the app, at submission and when an existing contributor next signs in, and a contributor's sightings are withheld from everything the build publishes, the map included, until they agree; a signed-in contributor still sees their own. Until the grant exists, nothing we originate can be served to a port system or contributed to Acartia on any stated terms. The options and who decides are in that issue; the rights policy is authoritative and would be amended by the outcome.

## Rejected

- **An SNS topic now.** The consumer that has asked wants an address to poll and a flag to filter on, not a subscription, and its developer is not known to be on AWS. Building a push channel ahead of a need for push picks the mechanism before the need.
- **Document the day files as the public contract instead of writing a new file.** It would bind the frontend's row shape, which decision 056 chose for the frontend's convenience, to outside readers. A separate file costs one more build task and keeps the two free to differ.
- **Re-broadcast Maplify as a real-time source.** Maplify's time is a report time, and the records are WRAS's own, coming back round five to fifteen minutes later. A feed that presented them as current observations would mislead exactly the consumer it was built for.
- **Leave it at the archive, or at Acartia's endpoint.** The archive is rebuilt for GBIF and says nothing about the present. Acartia's endpoint is the right short-term answer and Scott has given it; it carries one source, no observation time and no accuracy, and if this project is to succeed Acartia its consumers need somewhere to move to.

## Consequences

- The build gains one task and one file, and `docs/` gains a page describing the schema, the mapping from Acartia's fields and the latency (#738). The Stelis graph in `src/salishsea.rkt` names the task.
- The schema is versioned from its first publication, since outside readers will bind to it; a breaking change means a new version beside the old, not an edit in place.
- For an integration whose purpose is avoiding ships, what we publish is only as timely as our slowest hop and only as reliable as our build. Timeliness: Maplify's five-minute fetch is the hop we control; the rest is Whale Alert's, Acartia's and WRAS's. Reliability: the Sentry monitor and the heartbeat (decision 012) already say when the build or a source has stopped, and `/status/ingest-runs.json` is public, so a consumer can read our freshness rather than trust it. What "verified" means to a port system has to be stated in the file's terms, confirmed under decision 080, not left to the word.
- The rights policy is found to assert a license on native sightings that no contributor granted (#739). Correcting it means a terms page and agreement required at submission for the grant going forward; the same agreement asked of each existing contributor when they next sign in, their sightings withheld from the published files until then (656 records from eleven people, 567 of them Scott's, so the map loses most native sightings for as long as it takes him to click); the archive's native license and dataset rights changed in the export SQL; and the policy amended to say what happened. Until the grant exists, nothing we originate reaches a bridge.
- PRODUCT.md's out-of-scope entry for real-time push now cites this record, so the next person to propose a topic finds the reasoning and the condition under which it changes. PRODUCT.md does not yet say this project succeeds Acartia; that is Scott's expectation, and a decision of its own when it is made.
